# Ikigai Series — Part 2: Find Your Opportunity
Name: Solomon Wells   Niche: Small-group youth hockey skills training (Boise, Idaho)

## 1. Competitor Research
Strongest niche from Part 1: youth hockey skill development, narrowed to extra skills
coaching for Boise players ages 8–12. Five competitors (local + one online benchmark):

- Idaho IceWorld / Boise Skills Academy (BSA): Learn to Play through house league, plus
  BSA for extra practices, scrimmages, camps, and clinics year-round. BSA has an
  exclusive partnership with the City of Boise. Audience: most Boise youth players, 4–17.
- Mylymok camps (Idaho IceWorld): Three-day skill camps at Idaho IceWorld, $275 per
  player, coaches with pro and NCAA experience (2026 dates: July 17–19 and August 21–23).
- Next Level Hockey Idaho: 2,000 sq ft indoor synthetic-ice training space in Garden
  City; individual, semi-private, goalie, shooter, beginner, and elite training.
- IdaHockey LLC: Small goalie training pad with real ice in Boise for private and
  small-group goalie training, plus video review and mentorship.
- Online Hockey Training: Self-paced video courses, about $178 (from $249), lifetime
  access.

Market context: An Aspen Institute Project Play survey found hockey is the most expensive
youth sport, costing families more than $2,500 a year per player. A Boise parent guide
notes hockey in Boise is booming even though there are only a few rinks.

## 2. Gap Analysis
- Idaho IceWorld / BSA: Does well — clear development pathway, strong local trust,
  year-round clinics/camps, city partnership. Missing/weak — only two rinks so ice fills
  quickly and sessions can be crowded; could not find BSA clinic prices online.
- Mylymok camps: Does well — elite credentials, exciting name, deep player development.
  Missing/weak — only a few weekends a year, and $275 per camp is hard to repeat.
- Next Level Hockey Idaho: Does well — flexible formats in a dedicated space not
  dependent on rink ice. Missing/weak — brand new, limited name recognition, no public
  pricing/schedules found.
- IdaHockey LLC: Does well — specialized small-group and private goalie coaching.
  Missing/weak — goalies only, does not serve skaters.
- Online Hockey Training: Does well — affordable, self-paced, works anywhere.
  Missing/weak — video only, no live coaching, nobody corrects technique.

The gap: The market splits into expensive in-person options and cheap online options
with no feedback. The middle looks open: regular, affordable small-group coaching (4–6
kids) with a trusted coach and a clear monthly progress update, not tied to scarce rink
ice. The opening: no program found combines small groups, a low price, and regular
progress communication with parents.

## 3. Customer Conversation
Who: Robin, a hockey parent whose 10-year-old son plays competitive youth hockey.
How: Phone call on October 28, 2026. Context: worked with her son at a few camps, so she
knows me; treat her interest as encouraging rather than proof.

Key exchanges:
- "Team practices focus on everyone, so sometimes kids don't get the individual attention
  they need." — Robin
- She has tried camps, team clinics, and private lessons. Private lessons ~$50–$80 per
  session; camps a few hundred per week. "Sometimes it's hard to justify paying that much
  for only 30 or 60 minutes of extra training." — Robin
- On small groups of 4–6: "I think a group of four to six kids would be a good balance.
  They could still compete and learn from each other, but the coach would have enough
  time to work with everyone." — Robin
- Would "definitely consider" it; comfortable paying ~$25–$35 per session; prefers a
  package/monthly option. Progress updates: what he's doing well, what to improve
  (skating speed, shooting, stickhandling, confidence), a few things to practice at home.
- What would stop her: price, scheduling, trust. "Being a good hockey player doesn't
  automatically mean you're a good coach." — Robin. "I wouldn't want to drive 45 minutes
  across town for a one-hour session." — Robin. Recommendations/reviews from other parents
  would make her more likely to sign up.

What changed: Robin's point that a good player isn't automatically a good coach. After the
call: prioritize coaches experienced teaching younger players; keep pricing ~$25–$35 with
a monthly package; build in short monthly written progress updates; keep sessions close;
collect reviews from early families.

## 4. AI Stress Test
Used Claude (claude.ai) as devil's advocate.

Round 1 — challenges: ice access may be hard (two rinks; IceWorld–BSA exclusivity, private
lesson protocols); the math might not work ($25–$35 x 4–6 kids = ~$100–$210 per session
must cover space, coach pay, insurance, fees); could be an unneeded middleman (Next Level
already offers semi-private); trust cuts both ways (vetting, background checks,
certification, insurance); evidence is thin (one friendly parent); competitors could copy.

My rebuttal: biggest worries are affordable ice time and profitability; competition concern
overstated; could rent ice off-peak or use synthetic ice for stickhandling/shooting/puck
control; plan to ask Jackson Howell at Next Level Hockey about options and costs. What I
offer others don't: small groups of 4–6, affordable pricing, more parent communication —
another option, not a replacement.

Round 2 — Claude: agreed off-peak/synthetic ice are smart testable savers and Robin's
numbers leave room if costs stay low; pushed back that "personal experience" is too vague
(real differentiators are Robin's three asks: affordable price, trusted coach, progress
updates); competition only partly overstated (BSA could add small groups); listed gaps —
whether paid sessions can run at Idaho IceWorld, coach credentials/insurance, whether the
math works. Break-even (illustrative): $120/session cost -> 4 kids @ $30 breaks even,
6 kids @ $30 leaves $60.

## 5. My Decision
Build small-group (4–6 kids) hockey skills training with monthly parent progress reports
for Boise players ages 8–12, because parents like Robin find private lessons too expensive
and camps too crowded.

Why this one won: of three Part 1 finalists (youth hockey skill development, athlete
contract support, sports performance/recruiting analysis), youth hockey skill development
was most buildable and testable now; fits the hockey passion and coaching goal; clearest
gap; a real parent confirmed the problem and gave price/trust guidance.

Still to find out: ice/synthetic-ice costs and whether paid sessions can run at Idaho
IceWorld vs a space like Next Level Hockey; whether $25–$35 covers costs at 4–6 kids; coach
vetting (teaching experience, background checks, certification, insurance); whether more
parents beyond Robin would pay (pilot).
