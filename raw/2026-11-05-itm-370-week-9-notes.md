Reflection

Three things stood out to me from Noah's talk. First, he said you can't just be the
"human in the loop," you have to be an expert in the loop. AI is great at writing code,
but it only builds what you ask for in the moment and doesn't think about where the
project is going. If you keep adding features on top of a weak foundation, it
eventually breaks. He called that the "greenfield effect." So before I build anything
with AI, I'll spend more time planning and learning the basics, and I won't accept
answers I don't understand. Second, he said curiosity beats resistance. People who
avoid AI completely are the ones most likely to get caught off guard by it, and
employers are already saying they won't hire people who refuse to use it. I don't have
to love AI, but I should know what it can do, so I'll keep trying new tools. Third, I
liked what he said about not letting AI dull your creativity. When he first used Suno,
the songs felt shallow, but once he wrote his own lyrics and let AI turn them into
music, it felt like his own work. Since AI makes it so easy to get decent results, you
have to put in a little extra effort to make something feel like yours. I'll start with
my own ideas first and then use AI to build on them.

Question 3: a practical use case I could set up in a day

Noah said you could build a "dueling models" setup in about a day, and that's what I'd
do. I'd use two different AI models and give each a different personality, one critical
and one positive. Then I'd run my ideas, writing, or research through both and see
where they disagree. This would also help with the problem of AI just telling me my
idea is great, since one of them is built to push back. I'd ask AI to walk me through
setting it up, and I'd expect a few errors along the way, which Noah said is normal. In
the end I'd have a reusable "second opinion" tool I could use before turning in an
assignment or starting a project.
